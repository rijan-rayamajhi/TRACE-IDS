# Is It Transfer? Source Data Adds Nothing to Few-Shot Cross-Dataset Intrusion Detection on NetFlow

**Rijan Rayamajhi**

## 1. Introduction

Machine-learning (ML) network intrusion detection systems (NIDS) routinely report near-perfect accuracy on the dataset they were trained on, yet they fail when moved to a different network. Cross-dataset evaluations across the major public benchmarks show that models trained on one dataset detect attacks in another at rates far below their in-dataset performance (Cantone et al., 2024)<!--ref:cantone2024cross--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->, and this gap persists across model families, feature sets and benchmark pairs (Apruzzese et al., 2022<!--ref:apruzzese2022cross--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->; D'hooge et al., 2020<!--ref:dhooge2020inter--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->; Layeghy & Portmann, 2023<!--ref:layeghy2023explainable--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->). The difficulty is not new. Sommer and Paxson argued more than a decade ago that intrusion detection differs from other machine-learning domains precisely because operational traffic is diverse and the cost of errors is asymmetric (Sommer & Paxson, 2010)<!--ref:sommer2010outside--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->.

A practical remedy has emerged in recent work: when a detector is deployed on a new network, label a small amount of the new network's traffic and adapt the existing model to it. TAN-IDS, an evaluation framework for NetFlow-based detectors, reports that fine-tuning a source-trained model on 5% of labelled target data restores F1 to about 0.98 after zero-shot transfer collapses (Ha Thanh, 2026)<!--ref:hathanh2026tanids--><!--anchor:section:Table%2010-->. Hakim et al. report similar architecture-dependent recovery for lightweight Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT) detectors adapted with limited target data (Hakim et al., 2026)<!--ref:hakim2026crossdomain--><!--anchor:section:5.6-->, and a recent survey describes few-shot learning as a promising route to detection under label scarcity (Roszeitis et al., 2026)<!--ref:roszeitis2026fewshot--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->.

These results share an untested assumption: that the *source* dataset contributes to the recovered performance. Neither TAN-IDS nor Hakim et al. trains a model on the same small set of target labels alone. TAN-IDS compares its fine-tuned models only with zero-shot transfer and with training on the full target split, in a single run without significance tests (Ha Thanh, 2026)<!--ref:hathanh2026tanids--><!--anchor:section:6.2-->, and Hakim et al. note the missing small-sample-only condition themselves (Hakim et al., 2026)<!--ref:hakim2026crossdomain--><!--anchor:section:6.3-->. Without that control, recovery that the target labels would have produced on their own is credited to transfer.

The question matters beyond bookkeeping. Machine learning for security is prone to pitfalls in which a model succeeds for reasons unrelated to the task, including spurious correlations and inappropriate baselines (Arp et al., 2022)<!--ref:arp2022dos--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->. More broadly, deep models often learn shortcuts that hold on a benchmark but fail under distribution shift (Geirhos et al., 2020)<!--ref:geirhos2020shortcut--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->, and recent surveys of NIDS datasets document design limitations that make such shortcuts available (Goldschmidt & Chudá, 2025)<!--ref:goldschmidt2025network--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->. If cross-dataset recovery comes from the target labels, then the source dataset (and the transfer machinery built around it) adds cost and risk without benefit.

This paper tests the assumption directly. We ask: *when a detector moves to a new network and k target flows per class are labelled, does the source dataset add anything over training on those k flows alone, and why or why not?* We study the four NetFlow v3 datasets (NF-CICIDS2018-v3, NF-UNSW-NB15-v3, NF-ToN-IoT-v3 and NF-BoT-IoT-v3), which share one standardized feature set (Luay et al., 2025)<!--ref:luay2025temporal--><!--anchor:section:Table%201-->, so that every one of the 12 directed dataset pairs can be evaluated under one protocol.

Two design choices make the answer credible. First, every adaptation method is compared with a model trained on exactly the same target flows, drawn from the same unlabelled pool and evaluated on the same held-out test set, so any difference can only come from the source. Second, the comparison is repeated over 10 seeds and tested with paired statistics, rather than read from a single run in which differences of a few points can arise by chance.

Our contributions are:

1. **A leak-free, multi-seed protocol with a target-only control.** Normalization statistics come only from data visible at training time, few-shot samples are nested across label budgets, and every adaptation method is compared with a model trained on the same k target flows, over 10 seeds with paired tests (Section 3).
2. **No consistent benefit from the source.** Across 12 pairs, three model families and three ways of using the source (pooling, importance weighting and fine-tuning), the source helps in 34 of 540 paired comparisons (6%) and hurts in 153 (28%); with a shared scaling of both domains, 41 (8%) and 205 (38%) (Section 4).
3. **A non-reproduction and a budget check.** A published zero-shot F1 of 0.81 between UNSW-NB15 and CSE-CIC-IDS2018 does not reproduce on NetFlow v3, and at TAN-IDS's 5% label budget, target-only training matches source-based adaptation (Section 4).
4. **A mechanism.** A domain classifier separates every pair of networks with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of at least 0.996, including benign traffic and flows of the same attack category. On raw features, 23–40 of the 45 features must be removed before this falls below 0.9, so the separation is not one artifact column. Per-domain feature scaling, a common preprocessing step, inflates separability further and is a hidden confound (Section 5).
5. **A recommended evaluation protocol** for cross-dataset NIDS studies (Section 7).

Section 2 positions these contributions against prior work on cross-dataset generalization, few-shot adaptation and dataset artifacts.

## 2. Related Work

### 2.1 Cross-dataset generalization in NIDS

That ML-NIDS fail on unseen networks is well established. Cantone et al. train four classifiers on four CIC-family benchmarks, including a corrected release, and find near-perfect same-dataset accuracy but mostly near-chance cross-dataset accuracy, with visualizations exposing dataset anomalies that block generalization (Cantone et al., 2024)<!--ref:cantone2024cross--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->. Layeghy and Portmann evaluate seven supervised and unsupervised models across four benchmarks in a cross-domain setting. They find that no model generalizes across all of them and that generalization is strongly asymmetric between source and target. Shapley-value (SHAP) analysis attributes the failures to features tied to the class label in one dataset but not in the others (Layeghy & Portmann, 2023)<!--ref:layeghy2023explainable--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->. Earlier, D'hooge et al. showed that twelve supervised learners trained on CIC-IDS2017 generalize poorly to the closely related CSE-CIC-IDS2018, even on the shared denial-of-service (DoS) and botnet classes (D'hooge et al., 2020)<!--ref:dhooge2020inter--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->, and Apruzzese et al. formalized cross-evaluation of NIDS across labelled datasets as a way to expose risks that single-dataset evaluation hides (Apruzzese et al., 2022)<!--ref:apruzzese2022cross--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->.

Two recent preprints sharpen the picture. Atif and Ali compare classical models with a fine-tuned language model on the NetFlow v2 versions of UNSW-NB15 and CSE-CIC-IDS2018. They report a zero-shot UNSW→CIC F1 of 0.81 for XGBoost, but only after a staged removal of TTL, packet-length and TCP-window features; the opposite direction collapses to F1 0.02–0.07 for all models (Atif & Ali, 2026)<!--ref:atif2026threeaxis--><!--anchor:section:4.2-->. Their final feature set was selected using cross-dataset F1 as the arbiter (Atif & Ali, 2026)<!--ref:atif2026threeaxis--><!--anchor:section:3.3-->, which on our reading means target labels informed the choice of features. Hakim et al. train lightweight models on one IIoT testbed and test them zero-shot on two others, obtaining F1 of 0.09–0.28. SHAP shows that coarse port buckets act as a shortcut, so that coarsening a feature relocates rather than removes it (Hakim et al., 2026)<!--ref:hakim2026crossdomain--><!--anchor:section:5-->. Both studies treat cross-network failure as the starting point; neither isolates what the source contributes once target labels are available.

### 2.2 Few-shot and transfer adaptation for NIDS

The closest prior work is TAN-IDS, a deployment-oriented evaluation framework that maps UNSW-NB15 and NF-CSE-CIC-IDS2018 to a common eight-dimensional flow interface. It compares in-dataset, cross-dataset, mixed-domain and fine-tuning scenarios, and reports that fine-tuning on 5% labelled target data restores random-forest and XGBoost F1 to about 0.98 after cross-dataset collapse (Ha Thanh, 2026)<!--ref:hathanh2026tanids--><!--anchor:section:Table%2010-->. Fine-tuning is compared only with zero-shot transfer and with training on the full target split, however; in the sections and tables we checked (3.5, 4.2, 5.4, Tables 7–10) no model is trained on the 5% subset alone. The results also come from a single run without significance tests, which the author acknowledges (Ha Thanh, 2026)<!--ref:hathanh2026tanids--><!--anchor:section:6.2-->. Hakim et al. likewise compare adapted models only with their own zero-shot baseline, and note that a decision tree refit on the small target sample alone is a missing condition (Hakim et al., 2026)<!--ref:hakim2026crossdomain--><!--anchor:section:6.3-->. Sarhan et al. take a different route, mapping NetFlow features to semantic attributes so that unseen attack classes can be recognized zero-shot (Sarhan et al., 2023)<!--ref:sarhan2023zeroshot--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->. A recent systematic review of few-shot NIDS finds that most studies use five or fewer shots and the CIC benchmarks, and that missing parameters and code limit reproducibility (Roszeitis et al., 2026)<!--ref:roszeitis2026fewshot--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->.

Two studies do include a target-only baseline, and both point in the direction of our results. Xu et al. pretrain a multimodal few-shot detector on one CIC dataset and fine-tune it on the other. Their own tables show that source pretraining lowers accuracy on CICIDS2018 at every shot count, by 6.4 percentage points at K=5, and on CICIDS2017 at K=5 (Xu et al., 2025)<!--ref:xu2025multimodal--><!--anchor:section:Tables%208-9-->. The comparison, however, uses a near-domain pair from the same lab, a small test set (about 30 flows per class on our reading of their Table 7) and no repeated runs. Kim compares few-shot domain-adaptation methods with a target-only baseline between Linux syscall and Windows Sysmon logs. Target-only learning wins in one direction and loses in the other (Kim, 2026)<!--ref:kim2026prototype--><!--anchor:section:4-->, but with five seeds a two-sided Wilcoxon test cannot reach p < 0.05, and the data are host logs rather than network flows. To our knowledge, based on web searches for cross-dataset, few-shot and transfer NIDS work covering 2020–2026 and the reference lists of the closest studies, with every hit verified against Crossref, arXiv or OpenAlex, as of 11 October 2026, no prior NetFlow study isolates the source's contribution with a target-only control under repeated, statistically tested runs. TAN-IDS is the nearest prior work, and the difference is precisely that control.

### 2.3 Dataset artifacts and shortcut learning

A second line of work explains why cross-dataset numbers can mislead. Arp et al. catalogue pitfalls in machine learning for security, including spurious correlations, sampling bias and inappropriate baselines (Arp et al., 2022)<!--ref:arp2022dos--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->, and Geirhos et al. describe shortcut learning as the general phenomenon of models exploiting decision rules that work on benchmarks but not under shift (Geirhos et al., 2020)<!--ref:geirhos2020shortcut--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->. NIDS benchmarks offer many such shortcuts. Audits of CICIDS2017 find flaws in traffic generation, flow construction and labelling that change benchmark results (Engelen et al., 2021<!--ref:engelen2021troubleshooting--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->; Lanvin et al., 2023<!--ref:lanvin2023errors--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->). Flood et al. identify six recurring data-design smells across seven highly cited benchmarks (Flood et al., 2024)<!--ref:flood2024bad--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->, and a review of 89 public NIDS datasets documents common data limitations (Goldschmidt & Chudá, 2025)<!--ref:goldschmidt2025network--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->. Roy and Minu show that static identifiers and timestamps in an Internet of Things (IoT) benchmark let tree models reach near-perfect scores that vanish under session-disjoint splits (Roy & Minu, 2026)<!--ref:roy2026unmasking--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->, and Apruzzese et al. argue that research evaluations of ML for intrusion detection neglect deployment conditions (Apruzzese et al., 2023)<!--ref:apruzzese2023sok--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->.

The time-to-live (TTL) features of UNSW-NB15 are the best-documented single artifact. Sarhan et al. find that a single TTL-based feature reaches close to the classification performance of the full 38-feature set, attribute this to the placement of attacker and victim nodes in the testbed, and describe it as similar to "a 'hidden label'" (Sarhan et al., 2021)<!--ref:sarhan2021featureanalysis--><!--anchor:section:5.1.1-->. In their NetFlow v2 work the same authors drop TTL features from UNSW-NB15 and NF-UNSW-NB15-v2 because of their extreme correlation with the labels (Sarhan et al., 2022)<!--ref:sarhan2022standard--><!--anchor:section:4-->, and Atif and Ali describe TTL in NF-UNSW-NB15-v2 as encoding which machine generated the traffic (Atif & Ali, 2026)<!--ref:atif2026threeaxis--><!--anchor:section:3.3-->. The NetFlow v3 release keeps MIN_TTL and MAX_TTL as ordinary features (Luay et al., 2025)<!--ref:luay2025temporal--><!--anchor:section:Table%201-->, and within the sources we screened, none tests whether the artifact persists in v3; our ablation in Section 5 is such a test.

### 2.4 Transfer learning, negative transfer and covariate shift

Transfer learning uses knowledge from a source domain to improve learning in a related target domain, and it can fail: when domains are too dissimilar, source data can lower target performance, a phenomenon called negative transfer (Pan & Yang, 2010<!--ref:pan2010survey--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->; Zhang et al., 2023<!--ref:zhang2023negative--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->). Ben-David et al. bound the target error of a classifier by its source error plus a divergence between the domains, which can be estimated from unlabelled data, and analyse how to weight a small labelled target set against a large source set (Ben-David et al., 2010)<!--ref:bendavid2010theory--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->. When only the input distribution shifts, weighting source examples by the density ratio of target to source inputs corrects the training objective (Shimodaira, 2000<!--ref:shimodaira2000improving--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->; Sugiyama et al., 2007<!--ref:sugiyama2007covariate--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->). Domain-adversarial training builds on the same theory, learning features that a domain classifier cannot tell apart (Ganin et al., 2016)<!--ref:ganin2016domain--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->. These results predict when a source should help, and they supply two of our tools: importance weighting as an adaptation method, and a domain classifier as a measure of how far apart two networks are.

None of the work above trains on the same target labels alone under repeated, tested runs on standardized NetFlow data. Section 3 builds a protocol that does.

## 3. Data and Experimental Protocol

### 3.1 Datasets

We use the NetFlow v3 releases of four public benchmarks: NF-CICIDS2018-v3, NF-UNSW-NB15-v3, NF-ToN-IoT-v3 and NF-BoT-IoT-v3. The releases are regenerated from the original packet captures with one flow exporter and share one feature set: the 43 NetFlow v2 features plus flow timestamps and inter-arrival-time statistics (Luay et al., 2025)<!--ref:luay2025temporal--><!--anchor:section:Table%201-->; the release has since been published in IEEE Access (Luay et al., 2026)<!--ref:luay2026timematters--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->. This common schema follows the NetFlow standardization effort of Sarhan et al., whose earlier releases first gave the four benchmarks a shared feature set (Sarhan et al., 2021<!--ref:sarhan2021netflow--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->; Sarhan et al., 2022<!--ref:sarhan2022standard--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->). The underlying testbeds are UNSW-NB15 (Moustafa & Slay, 2015)<!--ref:moustafa2015unsw--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->, CSE-CIC-IDS2018, conventionally cited through the CIC generation methodology (Sharafaldin et al., 2018)<!--ref:sharafaldin2018toward--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->, the network portion of ToN_IoT (Moustafa, 2021<!--ref:moustafa2021tonnetwork--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->; Alsaedi et al., 2020<!--ref:alsaedi2020ton--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->) and BoT-IoT (Koroniotis et al., 2019)<!--ref:koroniotis2019bot--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->.

We drop identifier columns (source and destination Internet Protocol (IP) addresses and ports, flow start and end timestamps, and the attack-name column) because they encode the testbed rather than the traffic. We also drop the two second-direction byte columns, which are empty for many flows; removing the affected rows instead would have discarded 28% of the UNSW-NB15 attacks. This leaves 45 numeric features and a binary label. The large datasets are subsampled uniformly to about two million flows. NF-BoT-IoT-v3 is 99.7% attacks (Luay et al., 2025)<!--ref:luay2025temporal--><!--anchor:section:Table%202-->, so we keep all of its benign flows and subsample only its attacks; otherwise too few benign flows would remain to measure false alarms when it is the target. Table 1 summarizes the processed data. For the mechanism analysis, each dataset's attack families are mapped to shared categories (DoS, distributed DoS (DDoS), scanning, brute force, web, backdoor, botnet), with families that have no counterpart elsewhere kept separate.

### 3.2 Leak-free protocol

Every experiment uses one protocol, repeated for seeds 0–9. For each directed pair (source, target), the target is split 50/50, stratified by label, into an *unlabelled pool* and a *test set*. The source contributes a class-stratified sample of 100,000 flows, and the test set is subsampled to 200,000 flows. Few-shot training data are drawn from the pool: k attack and k benign flows for k ∈ {1, 5, 10, 50, 100}. The draws are nested, so the k = 10 set contains the k = 5 set, and differences across k are not due to resampling. The test set is used only for scoring.

Normalization statistics come only from data a deployed system could see at training time. In the main setting, *per-domain scaling*, the source sample is z-scored by its own mean and standard deviation and the target by those of its unlabelled pool. It uses no target labels. Because Section 5 shows that per-domain scaling is itself a confound, we repeat the full twelve-pair experiment with *shared scaling*: both domains are z-scored with one set of statistics computed from the source sample together with the unlabelled pool. For tree models, shared scaling is equivalent to using raw features, because the same monotone transform is applied to every row.

### 3.3 Models and adaptation methods

We use three model families: a random forest (RF; 100 trees), histogram gradient-boosted trees (GBT; 200 iterations) and a multilayer perceptron (MLP; hidden layers of 64 and 32 units). Tree ensembles remain strong on tabular data (Grinsztajn et al., 2022)<!--ref:grinsztajn2022why--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->, and the MLP represents the neural models used in the few-shot literature. Hyper-parameters are fixed in advance and identical across methods. With very few training flows, the default GBT minimum leaf size cannot split and would make target-only GBT a constant predictor, so it is scaled to one tenth of the training set (capped at 20).

Each model is trained in four ways:

- **Target-only:** the k labelled target flows per class alone. This is the control that prior work omits.
- **Pooled:** the source sample plus the k target flows, weighted so that each (domain, class) cell carries equal total weight. Without weighting, 100,000 source flows would drown a handful of target flows.
- **Importance-weighted (IW):** the pooled set, with source flows reweighted by the estimated density ratio of target to source inputs (Shimodaira, 2000<!--ref:shimodaira2000improving--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->; Sugiyama et al., 2007<!--ref:sugiyama2007covariate--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->). A logistic-regression domain classifier trained on source flows against the *unlabelled* target pool estimates the ratio, clipped to [0.01, 100]. With k = 0, this is unsupervised domain adaptation.
- **Fine-tuned:** a model pretrained on the source and then adapted to the k flows. The RF keeps its 100 source trees and grows 100 more on the target flows; the GBT uses the frozen source model as its initial prediction and fits 50 residual trees; the MLP continues gradient descent from the source weights for 50 epochs.

With k = 0, the pooled model is the source-only model, which gives the zero-shot result.

Two further checks target specific prior claims. First, we reconstruct the staged feature sets of Atif and Ali on v3: removing TTL, then packet-length, then TCP flag and window features, plus a variant that also drops the v3-only inter-arrival features to match their v2 schema (Atif & Ali, 2026)<!--ref:atif2026threeaxis--><!--anchor:section:3.3-->. We test zero-shot transfer between UNSW and CIC on both a balanced and a natural-rate test set. Second, we repeat the TAN-IDS setting of fine-tuning with 5% of the target training split (Ha Thanh, 2026)<!--ref:hathanh2026tanids--><!--anchor:section:3.5-->, adding the missing target-only arm.

### 3.4 Statistics

We report F1 on the attack class, together with recall, false-positive rate (FPR) and balanced accuracy (the mean of the true-positive and true-negative rates). Recall alone can be inflated by a model that flags everything. For every (pair, model, method, k) we compute the paired difference in F1 between the method and target-only over the 10 seeds and apply a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test (Wilcoxon, 1945)<!--ref:wilcoxon1945individual--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->. This is the test recommended for comparing two classifiers over repeated measurements (Demšar, 2006)<!--ref:demsar2006statistical--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->. A comparison is a *help* if p < 0.05 and the mean difference is positive, a *hurt* if p < 0.05 and it is negative, and *no difference* otherwise. With 12 pairs, 3 models, 3 methods and 5 values of k there are 540 comparisons. We also apply Holm's step-down correction across all 540 (Holm, 1979)<!--ref:holm1979simple--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->. With 10 seeds the smallest attainable p is 0.002, so the correction is strict, and we therefore interpret the uncorrected verdicts as a pattern rather than as individual findings.

### 3.5 Mechanism probes

To explain the transfer results, we measure how separable two networks are with a domain classifier: a gradient-boosted model trained to tell source flows from target flows. Its five-fold cross-validated AUC is 0.5 when the domains are indistinguishable and 1.0 when they are perfectly separable. A domain classifier is a standard way to characterize dataset shift (Rabanser et al., 2019)<!--ref:rabanser2019failing--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->, and it is the quantity that domain-adversarial training tries to drive down (Ganin et al., 2016)<!--ref:ganin2016domain--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->. We compute it on all flows, on benign flows only, and within each attack category the two datasets share (20,000 flows per side, 5 seeds). We also measure attack recall per category; the rank correlation between the feature importances of the source model and of an RF trained on 100,000 labelled target flows; and, in a greedy ablation, how many features must be removed (most-relied-on first, by permutation importance on held-out data) before the domain classifier's AUC falls below 0.95, 0.9, 0.75 and 0.6 (6 pairs, 3 seeds, 10,000 flows per side). Finally, we compute each feature's domain AUC alone, on raw values and after per-domain scaling.

## 4. Results: Does the Source Help?

### 4.1 One pair, one model

We begin with the setting closest to prior work: a random forest transferred between NF-CICIDS2018-v3 and NF-UNSW-NB15-v3 in both directions (Fig. 1). Adding the source (unweighted, or with equal weight per domain and class) does not improve on the target-only model in a consistent way. With 10 labelled flows per class, F1 for CIC→UNSW is 0.775 with the source, 0.832 with the weighted source and 0.811 with target-only training. For UNSW→CIC the figures are 0.530, 0.531 and 0.555. Differences of this size change sign across k and directions, and they are smaller than the seed-to-seed spread.

The one place where the source appears to win is misleading. At k = 1, target-only CIC→UNSW recall is far higher than recall with the source, but its FPR is 0.400 against 0.005. The target-only model labels a large share of benign traffic as attacks, and the source-trained model labels almost nothing as an attack. Neither is a usable detector, which is why we report FPR and balanced accuracy alongside recall.

### 4.2 Stronger models and adaptation methods

A random forest pooled with source data is a weak use of the source. We therefore give the source every reasonable chance: three model families and three adaptation methods, including importance weighting, which corrects for covariate shift, and fine-tuning, which is the method TAN-IDS uses. On CIC↔UNSW the 90 paired comparisons with target-only give zero cases where the source is significantly better and 25 where it is significantly worse; the remaining 65 show no difference. Under shared scaling the counts are 0 better and 38 worse (Fig. 2).

### 4.3 All twelve dataset pairs

Table 2 extends the comparison to all 12 directed pairs of the four datasets. Under per-domain scaling, the source helps in 34 of 540 comparisons (6%), makes no significant difference in 353 (65%) and hurts in 153 (28%). The median change in F1 from adding the source is −0.011, and the mean is −0.039. Under shared scaling the pattern strengthens: 41 helps (8%), 294 no differences (54%) and 205 hurts (38%), with a median change of −0.025. Harm outnumbers help for every method, every model and every k under both scalings. Fine-tuning is the most harmful method under per-domain scaling (72 hurts against 10 helps). The random forest is hurt most often under shared scaling (99 of 180 comparisons).

The harm depends on how many target labels are available. Under per-domain scaling, the median change in F1 is −0.015 at k = 1, −0.025 at k = 5 and −0.029 at k = 10. It then shrinks to −0.003 at k = 50 and to almost zero (−0.0002) at k = 100. The source does the most damage at exactly the small budgets where it is supposed to help, and it fades as target labels accumulate and outweigh it. Under shared scaling the median change stays negative at every k, from −0.037 at k = 5 to −0.017 at k = 100.

Fig. 3 shows the verdicts for each pair. Benefit is rare and pair-dependent. Under per-domain scaling the most favourable pair is ToN→CIC, with 9 helps and 5 hurts; Section 5 shows that this exception does not survive shared scaling. Under shared scaling the most favourable pair is BoT→CIC, with 14 helps and 16 hurts. Twelve of its helps occur at k ≤ 5 and all 16 hurts at k ≥ 10, so the source helps only until a few dozen target labels are available.

No individual comparison survives Holm correction over 540 tests in either setting: the smallest p-value, 0.002, is the floor attainable with 10 seeds. We therefore do not claim that the source significantly hurts in any particular cell. The claim the data support is narrower and, for practice, more important: across a large and varied set of conditions, the source gives no consistent benefit over training on the same target labels, and significant harm is four to five times as common as significant help.

### 4.4 Zero-shot transfer

Without any target labels, transfer is weak and depends on the pair. Under per-domain scaling, the best model's balanced accuracy is 0.66–0.71 on four pairs (BoT→CIC, ToN→BoT, UNSW→BoT and UNSW→ToN) and 0.61 on BoT→ToN. On the other seven it is at most 0.56, close to the 0.5 of chance. Under shared scaling the best pairs reach 0.68–0.76. In every pair and under both scalings, a target-only model trained on just 10 labelled flows per class does better than the best zero-shot model, with balanced accuracy of 0.78–0.94.

The zero-shot failures take two opposite forms, which recall alone would hide. A random forest trained on NF-BoT-IoT-v3, which is 97.5% attacks after sampling, flags almost every target flow: for BoT→CIC its recall is 1.000 at an FPR of 1.000. The same model trained on NF-CICIDS2018-v3 flags almost nothing in UNSW, ToN or BoT, with recall and FPR both at or near zero. In both cases balanced accuracy is 0.5, and the model has learned the source's base rate rather than any notion of an attack. The better zero-shot results come mostly from the MLP, for example UNSW→BoT with balanced accuracy 0.710 at an FPR of 0.156.

The strongest published zero-shot result on these datasets does not reproduce on v3. Atif and Ali report UNSW→CIC F1 of 0.81 with their pruned feature set C on NetFlow v2 (Atif & Ali, 2026)<!--ref:atif2026threeaxis--><!--anchor:section:4.2-->. With the same pruning reconstructed on v3, the best F1 is 0.21 on a balanced test set and 0.13 on a natural-rate test set. Across all their feature sets, balanced accuracy never exceeds 0.55 in either direction. Our replication differs in dataset version (v3 rather than v2), in models (RF and GBT rather than XGBoost) and in the exact feature lists, which we reconstructed from their description. Their feature set was also chosen using cross-dataset F1 on the target (Atif & Ali, 2026)<!--ref:atif2026threeaxis--><!--anchor:section:3.3-->, which would favour that pair.

### 4.5 The TAN-IDS label budget

Finally, we repeat the TAN-IDS setting, in which models are fine-tuned on 5% of the target training split (Ha Thanh, 2026)<!--ref:hathanh2026tanids--><!--anchor:section:3.5-->, and add the target-only arm it omits. At this budget (tens of thousands of labelled target flows) every method recovers. Target-only F1 is 0.959–1.000 across the two directions and three models, and pooling and fine-tuning are within ±0.006 of it, except for GBT fine-tuned UNSW→CIC, which is lower at 0.914. The recovery reported for source-aware fine-tuning is therefore matched by the target labels alone: at TAN-IDS's budget the source is not needed, and at small budgets (Sections 4.1–4.3) it does not help.

If the source carries no usable signal about the target, the next question is why. Section 5 examines what separates the networks.

## 5. Results: Why Doesn't the Source Help?

### 5.1 Every network is separable, even within an attack category

A domain classifier separates every pair of datasets almost perfectly. On raw features, the mean cross-validated AUC is at least 0.999 for every pair on all flows and on benign flows alone. It stays at least 0.996 within every attack category that two datasets share: DoS, DDoS, scanning, brute force and backdoor. The lowest value, 0.9966, is for DoS flows from two different networks. Under per-domain scaling the separation is the same or higher.

The within-category result matters most. If the source contained attacks that looked like the target's attacks, a model could learn them once and reuse them. Instead, a DoS flow from one testbed is almost always distinguishable from a DoS flow from another. Domain-adaptation theory bounds a classifier's target error by its source error plus a divergence between the domains (Ben-David et al., 2010)<!--ref:bendavid2010theory--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->. Near-perfect separability means that divergence is large, so a low source error says little about the target. Separability is also uniformly near its ceiling, so it cannot rank pairs: across the 12 pairs, the rank correlation between domain AUC and the mean source benefit is 0.06 (p = 0.85).

### 5.2 Shared attack types do not transfer

The per-category results confirm this. If the source transferred knowledge anywhere, it would be on attack categories it also contains. It does not. Without target labels, the source model's recall on categories present in the source is no higher than on categories it has never seen: 0.28 against 0.43 for the random forest and 0.26 against 0.31 for GBT (mean over pairs and seeds). Once 10 target flows per class are labelled, adding the source changes recall on shared categories by a median of −0.005 to −0.020, depending on model and method, against a median of about zero on unshared categories (Fig. 4). Knowing what DoS looks like on one network is no help in recognizing DoS on another.

Adding the source mainly makes the model more conservative. At k = 10, pooled training lowers the FPR on benign target flows from 0.163 to 0.133 for the random forest and from 0.197 to 0.149 for GBT, but it also lowers attack recall, so F1 does not improve. This is consistent with Table 2: the source shifts the decision threshold rather than adding attack knowledge.

Finally, the models attend to different features. The rank correlation between the feature importances of a random forest trained on the source and of one trained on 100,000 labelled target flows ranges from 0.25 to 0.83 across pairs. The highest overlaps are between ToN and CIC (0.83 in both directions), and ToN→CIC is the one pair where the source helped under per-domain scaling. Shared feature reliance may be a precondition for benefit, but with 12 pairs this is only a hypothesis.

### 5.3 Not one artifact column

A reviewer might suspect that the separability comes from one or two artifact features, such as the TTL fields documented for UNSW-NB15 (Sarhan et al., 2021)<!--ref:sarhan2021featureanalysis--><!--anchor:section:5.1.1-->. We tested this by greedy ablation. The domain classifier is refit after each step, and the feature it relies on most is removed. On raw features, MIN_TTL and MAX_TTL are among the first five features removed in 24 of 36 runs (6 pairs × 3 seeds × all/benign flows), yet removing the five most-relied-on features leaves the mean AUC at 0.999, and removing fifteen leaves it at 0.997 (Fig. 5). To push AUC below 0.9, 23–40 of the 45 features must be removed, depending on the pair (17–39 on benign flows only). Even with 30 features gone, the mean AUC is 0.86. The networks differ across most of the feature set, not in a handful of columns.

TTL does matter for detection, but not for the comparison we study. In the CIC↔UNSW experiment of Section 4.1, removing MIN_TTL and MAX_TTL lowers few-shot F1 for every method: at k = 5 for CIC→UNSW, from 0.700 to 0.542 with the source and from 0.697 to 0.500 with target-only training. Because both drop together, the source-versus-target comparison is unchanged. Within the sources we screened, this is the first test of the TTL artifact on the NetFlow v3 release, which still ships the fields (Luay et al., 2025)<!--ref:luay2025temporal--><!--anchor:section:Table%201-->.

### 5.4 Per-domain scaling is a hidden confound

The ablation also exposed a confound in a common preprocessing step. Per-domain z-scoring maps the same raw value to different numbers in different datasets. A categorical code such as `PROTOCOL` = 6 (TCP), or a column that is constant in one dataset, therefore lands on dataset-specific values, and a single such feature can then identify the dataset. On raw values, 13 of 270 single features (45 features × 6 pairs) separate two datasets with AUC ≥ 0.99; after per-domain scaling, 231 of 270 do. For CIC versus UNSW, `PROTOCOL` alone separates the datasets with AUC 0.565 on raw values but 1.000 after per-domain scaling. Under per-domain scaling, the domain classifier never falls below AUC 0.95 in the greedy ablation, even with 44 of 45 features removed.

This is why we repeated the twelve-pair experiment with shared scaling (Section 3.2). If per-domain scaling were hiding a real transfer benefit, shared scaling would reveal it. Instead, harm becomes more common (Table 2: 205 hurts against 153), and the one favourable pair under per-domain scaling, ToN→CIC, goes from 9 helps and 5 hurts to 0 helps and 18 hurts. The apparent benefit on that pair came from how per-domain scaling happened to align the two datasets, not from transferable knowledge. Per-domain scaling is a natural default, because in-dataset evaluations normalize each dataset on its own terms (Sarhan et al., 2022)<!--ref:sarhan2022standard--><!--anchor:section:4-->. Carried into cross-dataset work, the same habit produces this confound, and studies that adopt it should check whether it, rather than the traffic, is driving their results.

## 6. Discussion

### 6.1 Reconciling with prior recovery results

Our results do not contradict the recovery that TAN-IDS and Hakim et al. report; they reattribute it. Fine-tuning a source model on a few percent of target labels does restore detection (Ha Thanh, 2026)<!--ref:hathanh2026tanids--><!--anchor:section:Table%2010-->, and at the TAN-IDS budget we observe the same recovery. A model trained on those target labels alone recovers just as well, however, and at small budgets it usually does better than models that also use the source. The recovery is what the target labels deliver; the source's contribution, measured against that control, is absent or negative. The two studies in our survey that did include a target-only arm point the same way. Xu et al.'s source pretraining lowers few-shot accuracy at the smallest budget in both of their transfer directions (Xu et al., 2025)<!--ref:xu2025multimodal--><!--anchor:section:Tables%208-9-->. Kim finds target-only learning competitive with, and in one direction better than, every adaptation method tested on host logs (Kim, 2026)<!--ref:kim2026prototype--><!--anchor:section:4-->. Different data, models and adaptation methods converge on one finding: in cross-network intrusion detection, few target labels go further on their own than prior work assumed, and the source adds less.

### 6.2 Negative transfer is the expected outcome here

From the perspective of transfer-learning theory, the result is unsurprising. Transfer helps when the domains are close enough that source error is informative about target error (Ben-David et al., 2010)<!--ref:bendavid2010theory--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->. When they are far apart, source data can pull the model away from the target's decision boundary: negative transfer (Pan & Yang, 2010<!--ref:pan2010survey--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->; Zhang et al., 2023<!--ref:zhang2023negative--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->). Section 5 shows that the four NetFlow v3 networks are about as far apart as a domain classifier can measure, including within shared attack categories. Importance weighting cannot bridge this gap, because it assumes the source covers the target's input distribution (Shimodaira, 2000)<!--ref:shimodaira2000improving--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->. Our importance weights show the consequence. Under shared scaling they collapse onto a few source flows: the effective sample size is 0.1–3.8% of the source sample across pairs (median 0.8%). Per-domain scaling removes mean and scale differences first, and leaves 5–68% (median 34%), but the weighted source still does not help (Table 2). Our measurement of separability is coarse, however: a domain AUC near 1.0 for every pair cannot say *which* pairs will transfer, and finer measures of domain distance are needed for that.

### 6.3 When might a source help?

The exceptions in our data suggest where a source could still add value. Under shared scaling, the BoT→CIC helps all occur at k ≤ 10, and twelve of the fourteen at k ≤ 5. That is, the source helps only when the target model has almost nothing to learn from. The pair with the highest feature-importance overlap (ToN→CIC) was also the only favourable pair under per-domain scaling, although that benefit disappeared under shared scaling. Two hypotheses follow, both untested here: a source may help when target labels are extremely scarce, and when source and target models rely on the same features. Each could be checked before a source is used, with a small target-only pilot or a comparison of feature importances.

### 6.4 What this means for NIDS benchmarks

Our results also bear on how cross-dataset evaluations are read. If each benchmark occupies its own region of feature space, a cross-dataset score mostly measures how well the target labels are used. Benchmark construction plausibly contributes. The NetFlow v3 release documents that NF-UNSW-NB15-v3 contains only 40 distinct source and 40 destination IP addresses, that NF-BoT-IoT-v3 has only 20 source addresses, and that most attack classes in the other datasets were run on separate days (Luay et al., 2025)<!--ref:luay2025temporal--><!--anchor:section:Tables%204-5-->. Small host populations and separated capture periods are the kind of design choice that, as audits of other benchmarks show, leave learnable traces of how a dataset was made (Flood et al., 2024<!--ref:flood2024bad--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->; Goldschmidt & Chudá, 2025<!--ref:goldschmidt2025network--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->). The pitfalls Arp et al. describe, in particular spurious correlations and inappropriate baselines, apply directly to transfer claims made without a target-only arm (Arp et al., 2022)<!--ref:arp2022dos--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->.

### 6.5 Limitations

Our conclusions are bounded by the study design. We use four public testbed datasets, all in one NetFlow format. The four testbeds are themselves the source of the separability we measure, so the result may differ between real production networks that share infrastructure. We study binary detection only. We test three model families with fixed hyper-parameters; deep sequence models, meta-learning and domain-adversarial training are not included, although the separability we measure is the quantity such methods would need to overcome. Ten seeds give enough power to reveal a consistent pattern, but not to make any single comparison significant after correcting for 540 tests. Our replication of Atif and Ali reconstructs their feature sets from their description, uses v3 rather than v2, and uses RF and GBT in place of XGBoost. The evaluation is offline; real-time deployment and adversarial robustness are out of scope.

## 7. Recommended Protocol

Our findings suggest six practices for cross-dataset and few-shot NIDS studies:

1. **Always report target-only at the same label budget.** Any claim that a source dataset helps must be measured against a model trained on the same target labels alone. Without this control, transfer and target learning cannot be told apart. In our data the control changes the conclusion: recovery that looks like transfer is matched by the target labels alone.
2. **Use repeated runs and paired tests.** Report results over multiple seeds with paired tests, and correct for the number of comparisons. Single-run differences of a few points are within seed-to-seed noise, and a significance test needs enough seeds to be able to reject at all: with five seeds, a two-sided Wilcoxon test cannot reach p < 0.05.
3. **Report false-positive rate or balanced accuracy, not recall alone.** Few-shot and zero-shot models often reach high recall by flagging benign traffic; one of our zero-shot models reaches recall 1.0 at an FPR of 1.0.
4. **Take normalization statistics only from training-visible data, and check the scaling.** Never use target test data for normalization. Compare per-domain and shared scaling, because per-domain scaling can by itself make datasets separable.
5. **Report domain separability.** A domain classifier's AUC on benign traffic and within shared attack categories shows how much a source can plausibly contribute. An AUC near 1.0 within the same attack category warns that the source's attack examples may not resemble the target's.
6. **Ablate known artifacts.** Report results with and without documented artifact features such as TTL, and check whether separability survives their removal.

## 8. Conclusion

Recent work credits source datasets with recovering intrusion detection on new networks once a few target flows are labelled. On the four NetFlow v3 benchmarks, under a leak-free protocol with 10 seeds, the target labels alone achieve that recovery. Across 540 paired comparisons, three model families and three adaptation methods, the source gives no consistent benefit, and significant harm is four to five times as common as significant help, under both per-domain and shared scaling. Zero-shot transfer is weak and pair-dependent, and a published strong zero-shot result does not reproduce on v3. The reason is that each network occupies its own region of feature space, even for the same attack type, across most features rather than a single artifact. Per-domain scaling adds a further, avoidable source of separability. Future work should test whether these findings hold on production networks that share infrastructure and in multi-class detection, and should develop measures of domain distance fine enough to predict when a source will help. Until then, the safest default for practitioners is to label a small sample of their own traffic and train on it.
