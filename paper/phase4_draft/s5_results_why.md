## 5. Results: Why Doesn't the Source Help?

### 5.1 Every network is separable, even within an attack category

A domain classifier separates every pair of datasets almost perfectly. On raw features, the mean cross-validated AUC is at least 0.999 for every pair on all flows and on benign flows alone. It stays at least 0.996 within every attack category that two datasets share (DoS, DDoS, scanning, brute force and backdoor), although some categories are small on one side (188–213 backdoor flows and as few as 226 DoS flows per side); across seeds, no within-category AUC varies by more than 0.003 (standard deviation). The classifier itself does not manufacture separability: on two random halves of one dataset it gives 0.49–0.51.

The within-category result matters most. If the source contained attacks that looked like the target's, a model could learn them once and reuse them. Instead, a DoS flow from one testbed is almost always distinguishable from a DoS flow from another. Domain-adaptation theory bounds a classifier's target error by its source error plus a divergence between the domains (Ben-David et al., 2010)<!--ref:bendavid2010theory--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->, and near-perfect separability means that divergence is large, so a low source error guarantees little about the target. This is consistent with the absence of benefit but does not prove it, because separability concerns the inputs only, not whether two networks label similar inputs alike. Separability is also uniformly near its ceiling, so it cannot rank pairs: across the 12 pairs, the rank correlation between raw domain AUC and the mean source benefit under shared scaling is 0.03 (p = 0.93).

### 5.2 Shared attack types do not transfer

If the source transferred knowledge anywhere, it would be on attack categories it also contains. Under per-domain scaling (this analysis was not repeated under shared scaling), the source model's zero-shot recall on categories present in the source is no higher than on categories it has never seen: 0.28 against 0.43 for the random forest and 0.26 against 0.31 for GBT. Once 10 target flows per class are labelled, adding the source changes recall on shared categories by a median of −0.005 to −0.020, against about zero on unshared categories (Fig. 4). Pooling makes the model more conservative: at k = 10 it lowers the FPR on benign target flows from 0.163 to 0.133 for the random forest and from 0.197 to 0.149 for GBT, but also lowers attack recall. Fine-tuning instead raises the FPR, to 0.183 and 0.226. Neither adds attack knowledge that the target labels lack.

The models also attend to different features. The rank correlation between the feature importances of a random forest trained on the source and one trained on 100,000 labelled target flows ranges from 0.25 to 0.83 across pairs; each forest is trained within one domain, so this measure does not depend on the scaling. Feature overlap does not predict benefit. The highest overlap is between ToN and CIC (0.83), and ToN→CIC is favourable only under per-domain scaling, while UNSW→BoT, the most favourable pair under shared scaling, has the lowest overlap (0.25).

### 5.3 Not one artifact column

The separability might come from one or two artifact features, such as the TTL fields documented for UNSW-NB15 (Sarhan et al., 2021)<!--ref:sarhan2021featureanalysis--><!--anchor:section:5.1.1-->. We tested this by greedy ablation, refitting the domain classifier after removing the feature it relies on most. On raw features, MIN_TTL and MAX_TTL are among the first five features removed in 24 of 36 runs (6 pairs × 3 seeds × all/benign flows), yet on all flows, removing the five most-relied-on features leaves the mean AUC at 0.999, and removing fifteen leaves it at 0.997 (0.999 and 0.988 on benign flows; Fig. 5). To push AUC below 0.9, 23–40 of the 45 features must be removed (17–39 on benign flows only), and with 30 features gone the mean AUC is still 0.86. The networks differ across most of the feature set.

Nor does TTL hide a transfer benefit. Under per-domain scaling (our sensitivity setting), removing MIN_TTL and MAX_TTL from the CIC↔UNSW experiment of Section 4.1 turned the comparison in the source's favour: the weighted source beat target-only CIC→UNSW by 0.167 F1 at k = 5 and 0.147 at k = 10 (p = 0.037 and 0.014). Under shared scaling the same ablation shows the opposite, with the weighted source 0.263 F1 below target-only at k = 5 (p = 0.002). Repeating the full twelve-pair experiment without TTL under shared scaling, the source does slightly worse than with TTL: 42 helps and 228 hurts (against 41 and 205), 35 and 254 on balanced accuracy, and a mean change of −0.056 F1 (95% CI −0.080 to −0.030). Five pairs improve without TTL, most of all UNSW→BoT (from 9 helps and 3 hurts to 15 and none), while the other seven get worse; CIC→UNSW goes from 0 helps and 18 hurts to 0 and 13. Removing the artifact changes which pairs benefit, not the overall result. Within the sources we screened, this is the first test of the TTL artifact on the NetFlow v3 release, which still ships the fields (Luay et al., 2025)<!--ref:luay2025temporal--><!--anchor:section:Table%201-->.

### 5.4 Per-domain scaling corrupts separability measurements

Per-domain z-scoring maps the same raw value to different numbers in different datasets. A categorical code such as `PROTOCOL` = 6 (TCP), or a column that is constant in one dataset, therefore lands on dataset-specific values, and a tree can identify the dataset from that one value. On raw values, 13 of 270 single features (45 features × 6 pairs) separate two datasets with AUC ≥ 0.99; after per-domain scaling, 231 of 270 do. For CIC versus UNSW, `PROTOCOL` alone gives AUC 0.565 on raw values but 1.000 after per-domain scaling, and under per-domain scaling the domain AUC never falls below 0.95 in the greedy ablation, even with 44 of 45 features removed. The effect is strong enough that two random halves of the *same* dataset, each z-scored on its own, are separated with AUC 1.00: their means differ slightly, so each discrete value is shifted by a slightly different amount, and gradient-boosted trees split on the exact values. Separability measured after per-domain scaling therefore reflects the preprocessing, not the traffic, and we report it on raw features only.

For transfer models the picture is mixed. For continuous features, per-domain scaling aligns the means and spreads of the two domains, a mild form of domain alignment: the linear domain classifier used for importance weighting finds the domains less separable, and the weights' effective sample size is a median 34% of the source sample, against 0.8% under shared scaling. This aligned version transfers slightly better (153 hurts against 205), yet still gives no consistent benefit. Its one favourable pair, ToN→CIC, goes from 9 helps and 5 hurts to 0 helps and 18 hurts under shared scaling, so that benefit depended on how the preprocessing mapped the two datasets' codes. We therefore take shared scaling, which leaves every code where it is, as the main setting.
