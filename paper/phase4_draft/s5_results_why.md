## 5. Results: Why Doesn't the Source Help?

### 5.1 Every network is separable, even within an attack category

A domain classifier separates every pair of datasets almost perfectly. On raw features, the mean cross-validated AUC is at least 0.999 for every pair on all flows and on benign flows alone. It stays at least 0.996 within every attack category that two datasets share: DoS, DDoS, scanning, brute force and backdoor. The lowest value, 0.9966, is for DoS flows from two different networks. Under per-domain scaling the separation is the same or higher.

The within-category result matters most. If the source contained attacks that looked like the target's attacks, a model could learn them once and reuse them. Instead, a DoS flow from one testbed is almost always distinguishable from a DoS flow from another. Domain-adaptation theory bounds a classifier's target error by its source error plus a divergence between the domains (Ben-David et al., 2010)<!--ref:bendavid2010theory--><!--anchor:section:abstract-->. Near-perfect separability means that divergence is large, so a low source error says little about the target. Separability is also uniformly near its ceiling, so it cannot rank pairs: across the 12 pairs, the rank correlation between domain AUC and the mean source benefit is 0.06 (p = 0.85).

### 5.2 Shared attack types do not transfer

The per-category results confirm this. If the source transferred knowledge anywhere, it would be on attack categories it also contains. It does not. Without target labels, the source model's recall on categories present in the source is no higher than on categories it has never seen: 0.28 against 0.43 for the random forest and 0.26 against 0.31 for GBT (mean over pairs and seeds). Once 10 target flows per class are labelled, adding the source changes recall on shared categories by a median of −0.005 to −0.020, depending on model and method, against a median of about zero on unshared categories (Fig. 4). Knowing what DoS looks like on one network is no help in recognizing DoS on another.

Adding the source mainly makes the model more conservative. At k = 10, pooled training lowers the FPR on benign target flows from 0.163 to 0.133 for the random forest and from 0.197 to 0.149 for GBT, but it also lowers attack recall, so F1 does not improve. This is consistent with Table 2: the source shifts the decision threshold rather than adding attack knowledge.

Finally, the models attend to different features. The rank correlation between the feature importances of a random forest trained on the source and of one trained on 100,000 labelled target flows ranges from 0.25 to 0.83 across pairs. The highest overlaps are between ToN and CIC (0.83 in both directions), and ToN→CIC is the one pair where the source helped under per-domain scaling. Shared feature reliance may be a precondition for benefit, but with 12 pairs this is only a hypothesis.

### 5.3 Not one artifact column

A reviewer might suspect that the separability comes from one or two artifact features, such as the TTL fields documented for UNSW-NB15 (Sarhan et al., 2021)<!--ref:sarhan2021featureanalysis--><!--anchor:section:5.1.1-->. We tested this by greedy ablation. The domain classifier is refit after each step, and the feature it relies on most is removed. On raw features, MIN_TTL and MAX_TTL are among the first five features removed in 24 of 36 runs (6 pairs × 3 seeds × all/benign flows), yet removing the five most-relied-on features leaves the mean AUC at 0.999, and removing fifteen leaves it at 0.997 (Fig. 5). To push AUC below 0.9, 23–40 of the 45 features must be removed, depending on the pair (17–39 on benign flows only). Even with 30 features gone, the mean AUC is 0.86. The networks differ across most of the feature set, not in a handful of columns.

TTL does matter for detection, but not for the comparison we study. In the CIC↔UNSW experiment of Section 4.1, removing MIN_TTL and MAX_TTL lowers few-shot F1 for every method: at k = 5 for CIC→UNSW, from 0.700 to 0.542 with the source and from 0.697 to 0.500 with target-only training. Because both drop together, the source-versus-target comparison is unchanged. Within the sources we screened, this is the first test of the TTL artifact on the NetFlow v3 release, which still ships the fields (Luay et al., 2025)<!--ref:luay2025temporal--><!--anchor:section:Table%201-->.

### 5.4 Per-domain scaling is a hidden confound

The ablation also exposed a confound in a common preprocessing step. Per-domain z-scoring maps the same raw value to different numbers in different datasets. A categorical code such as `PROTOCOL` = 6 (TCP), or a column that is constant in one dataset, therefore lands on dataset-specific values, and a single such feature can then identify the dataset. On raw values, 13 of 270 single features (45 features × 6 pairs) separate two datasets with AUC ≥ 0.99; after per-domain scaling, 231 of 270 do. For CIC versus UNSW, `PROTOCOL` alone separates the datasets with AUC 0.565 on raw values but 1.000 after per-domain scaling. Under per-domain scaling, the domain classifier never falls below AUC 0.95 in the greedy ablation, even with 44 of 45 features removed.

This is why we repeated the twelve-pair experiment with shared scaling (Section 3.2). If per-domain scaling were hiding a real transfer benefit, shared scaling would reveal it. Instead, harm becomes more common (Table 2: 205 hurts against 153), and the one favourable pair under per-domain scaling, ToN→CIC, goes from 9 helps and 5 hurts to 0 helps and 18 hurts. The apparent benefit on that pair came from how per-domain scaling happened to align the two datasets, not from transferable knowledge. Per-domain scaling is a natural default, because in-dataset evaluations normalize each dataset on its own terms (Sarhan et al., 2022)<!--ref:sarhan2022standard--><!--anchor:section:4-->. Carried into cross-dataset work, the same habit produces this confound, and studies that adopt it should check whether it, rather than the traffic, is driving their results.
